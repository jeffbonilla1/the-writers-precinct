Det. Dale Hawthorne
20 Years Homicide

Do banks cooperate with a financial investigation, or do you need a warrant?

Let me tell you something about banks and financial records that took me about six years to fully appreciate: banks don't actually give a damn about your suspect's privacy. What they care about is their legal exposure. There's a meaningful difference between those two things, and understanding it is what separates a detective who can actually build a financial case from one who spends three weeks waiting on paperwork while the money disappears to the Cayman Islands.

Under the Right to Financial Privacy Act and general Fourth Amendment doctrine, you technically need a subpoena, a court order, or a grand jury subpoena to compel a bank to hand over records. A full search warrant is actually overkill most of the time — subpoenas are the workhorse here, because you don't need probable cause, you need relevance and an ongoing investigation. That bar is considerably easier to clear.

In the preliminary stages, before you've got solid ground under your feet, there's what I call the informal ask — a conversation with the bank's security department or law enforcement liaison. You're not demanding records, you're asking questions. Banks file Suspicious Activity Reports with FinCEN independent of any police investigation, and sometimes a bank's compliance department has already done half your work before you knocked on their door. I once caught a money laundering operation because a bank teller in Cincinnati had already reported the pattern six weeks before we showed up.

Here's your hard-won piece of advice, and I want you to tattoo it somewhere you'll see it: the most powerful financial investigative tool in crime fiction — and in real life — is almost never the dramatic bank record reveal. It's the gap between what someone earns and how they live. That gap is visible without a single subpoena. Your detective notices the car, the watch, the vacation photos on social media, the house in a neighborhood that doesn't match the job. That's your hook. That's what gets the wheels moving. The paperwork confirms what a good investigator already suspects. Write that moment of noticing the gap — not the moment of getting the records — and you'll have something that feels true, because it is.
